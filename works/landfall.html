They made landfall on a dreary winter morn, the sort where mist hangs heavy in the air and the sky is darkened by a gray that invades your soul. It was against this backdrop of apathetic despair that the transwhip shuttles were deployed from the great blimps suspended in the sky, making their way towards blood and slaughter. The men, once labeled children, then men, and now soldiers, nervously prepared for the spittubes to eject them from the craft at supersonic and enter warfare—many for the first time. And so they performed those superficial actions meant to calm and reassure, checking and rechecking their plasma guns, adjusting their standard-issue webform suits and starprint vests, resheathing heatknives. Many of them felt crushed by the weight of suspense, anticipation, fear. And while it is true that one cannot be brave without fear, it is also true that fear begets fear—and in this case, the fear was more of a crushing weight than a boon.

The war was one of those wars, led by a messiah, a leader whose men followed him without question or doubt. The leader in question was one of those rare men who move the path of history, on whom the rolling ball of history waits, every so patiently, for a simple push. Yes, for when religion and politics move in the same cart, there is not much that can stop it.

Yet the men were not religious fanatics, did not follow the messiah out of a piercing belief, did not love him as believers love saints or martyrs. They followed him because they had been told that the world beyond these shores would burn unless they arrived with guns and faith strapped to their backs. They followed because to refuse was to be branded a traitor to their families, to their homeland, to the very notion of decency. They followed because the older men had told the younger that this was what men do: they march, they fight, and if destiny requires it, they die. And so the soldiers descended not with conviction, but with resignation—the numb, hollow resignation of workers in a factory of death, cogs in a machine too ancient and too terrible to disassemble.

And so when the spittubes fired, hurling bodies headlong into the frigid air, the men did not shout and roar with zeal as the propaganda reels liked to pretend. Their boots struck the frozen earth not with triumph, but with that old, familiar sense of surrender—the quiet and wretched understanding that whatever came next was no longer in their hands. Plasma fire erupted before many could orient themselves, red and orange beams slicing through the mist like threads of fate. Screams rose, but no one remembered later whether they came from mouths or from memory.

In the chaos, among the confusion and the smoke and the metallic smell of ions scorching the air, one soldier stumbled forward—one of thousands, indistinguishable except to those who once loved him. And then—

He felt a blossoming pain in his gut and looked down to see red spreading rapidly from a hole in his uniform, once so spotless and clean—on the day his mother had kissed him on the cheek and looked him in the eyes and told him to come home. To please come home. On the day his father had told him he was the true man of the house now, going off to fight for his country. For freedom. On the day he had held his love near, had told her to watch the dog and herself, had told her he would be back in no time. On that day, so long ago and yet so close he might have plucked it off the shelf of his memories and held it near.

And with that final thought, performed and translated from electrical impulses running through physical material into the metaphysical in but a snap of the fingers or the blink of an eye, he fell to his knees, inexorable and inevitable as the fall of a colossus who had once stood proud, guarding with a watchful eye the people he was sworn to protect. His gun slipped from his shaking hands, unneeded and unheeded.

Mama, he whispered. As plaintively as a babe in the crook of his mother’s loving arms. And as a child would have done, he reached his arms upwards, searching perhaps for the divine, but most likely, rather, for his Divine Maternity.

And he was beautiful on that battlefield, unarmed and undone, red spreading from his abdomen but never staining his soul. Eyes burned with an undimmed passion, undeterred by the hastening of Death’s cold embrace, as they stared into the heavens and saw the herald of Death arriving to collect a long-unpaid debt. Yes, he was beautiful before a shell erased his body and his promise to his mother.

And the last thing left, the only thing his mother received from the Army in exchange for a son’s promise and a son’s life and a son’s dreams, was a ring. A beautiful ring shaped in an intricate spiral, wrought of iron and sweat and tears and a dream of a shared life. A ring with a blood-red ruby on top.
