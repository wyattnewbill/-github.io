Dawnlight streamed through the window as Sansa V was woken from a fevered haze of half-remembered dreams and memories. He woke with the peculiar sense of having remembered something important, only for it to be forgotten again. But no matter, for it was day again, and with it the other ones slipped away, unimportant and lost in the murk of what was and what was to be.

His bleary eyes took in the motes of dust floating in the air from the old, creaking cot on which he slept. His joints creaked in response as he levered himself upwards, so that he could grasp his coat and worn cap from the hook on the wall. He slung on his coat and set the cap on his bed, before he bent over to put on his shoes, lacing them up slowly as his stiff, cold fingers warmed up. Sansa stood then, grabbing his cap and donning it with two hands, one in front and one in back. He nodded, sweeping a stray strand of graying, peppered hair behind an ear, before grabbing the shovel from its place by the door and going outside to begin his day.

It must be noted, here, that Sansa V was not an ordinary man—certainly, he had perhaps lived most of an ordinary life, was ordinarily built, ordinarily conceived, even ate ordinarily; but it was his profession that was not ordinary.

Sansa V was a Switchman.

A Switchman is an unusual profession. You likely don't think about it, after all. You simply move through life, taking the trains as they come. But the Switchman is the one on whom the trains move—with a single switch, he can change the entire course of the trains. One heading from Danvsk to Slattern? With a simple twitch by Sansa, you would be on your way to the Nisle and the desert.

Sansa was quite happy with this fact, too—whenever Control hailed him on the radiophone, he would tell them, with great pride, of his exploits as a Switchman. And Control would smile—or, at least, as much a smile as one could give through a radiophone—and tell him good job, as a parent would.

But not many thoughts of this nature ran through Sansa’s head as he shoveled the path in front of his hut—not many thoughts whatsoever. He was mostly concerned with the snow that had built up over the night, and now dusted the path the Deliveryman, Josef, would take to his shack. I must keep the path clean for Josef, Sansa thought. Also running through his mind: Perhaps there will be a train to Sens today.

Sansa stopped suddenly, however, his breath coming in great puffs of frost, as the sunlight began to stream through the trees and strike the path and the fresh snow in great streams. Rays of pure light leaped off the snow like trout in summer, and, Sansa concluded, the sun’s heat would soon take care of the path for him.

What’s more, he was beginning to feel the heat through his black wool coat. Ah, but the rails! his mind suddenly exclaimed. He gazed at the sun once more, his sun-leathered face set in clear relief, before hurrying to shovel the rails before the sun did his job for him—and before the trains came his way, and his day started in earnest once more.

Sansa whistled a little ditty as he shoveled his part of the rails, something that he had heard on the radio a couple nights prior. It had been sung by a diminutive little lady with the most sweet, clear voice, and it went something like:

Little love, little love
How I sing to you
Sweet love, sweet love
How I laugh for you
How would I choose
Between the two of you
If only I could choose
Then I would be free

It was quite a nice song, and it reminded Sansa a lot of a woman he had dated many years ago, and who had been lost in that fevered haze of memories before the song had brought her to the forefront of his mind. She had auburn hair and sun-burnished skin, and the sweetest laugh, and he smiled to think of her. She had made her choice, though—it was the man from Danvsk, who was now her husband—all because he was rich and Sansa was, well, not.

Sansa supposed it didn’t matter all that much, anymore.

But the woman only ran through Sansa’s mind once before rushing back into the wellspring of memories and dreams he had amassed over a life of experience. And so he whistled his ditty as he shoveled, back and forth, back and forth. He made sure to get in between the ties and to knock the snow off the web of the rail—he was, after all, a Switchman, and Switchmen were held to high standards.

At least, Sansa held himself to a high standard.

His cap slipped down over his eyes a little, and as he lifted it back up the sun streamed onto his face, hot and bright. It felt, to Sansa, a little wet, and he supposed that maybe the sun was making him tear up. No matter, he was mostly finished anyway. He lifted his shovel and set it over his shoulder, before turning and trudging back towards his shack. His steps made that wonderful crunch-swish that comes with a pure snowfall, and his breaths came faster and mistier now. Sweat trickled down his forehead towards his brow, and he stopped to wipe it off.

Before entering his cabin, though, Sansa turned to survey his domain—the railroad, freshly cleaned. He decided to go inside and make himself a cup of tea before beginning his work, and it was now that the first puffs of smoke from a train appeared over the tops of the snow-strung trees in the distance. The rail wound through the forest before coming to Sansa’s shack, before continuing in a twisting, convoluted path outwards towards its destination, whatever that may be. In fact, Sansa’s portion of the railroad was the only one perfectly straight, for an entire furlong.

Sansa liked to believe the whole railroad was straight.

And so he entered his cabin, shaking the snow off his boots as the trains meandered along the tracks, winding along the snow and the trees towards Sansa’s freshly cleaned rails.
