The Earth is dying.

It’s a simple fact, Lucia reflects, sitting in the backseat of an armored car winding its way through the shattered boulevard. Screams and shouts twist around the car like the smoke from the fires engulfing the buildings on the street—smoke pouring into the air as the flames destroy anything that hasn’t been turned to dust by the atomics, running rampant through the city in sync with the people (if they can even be called people). People rioting, screaming, hating, dismantling what is left of poor society. The anger and loathing can almost be felt, like a disgusting scent—it makes Lucia want to gag, even though she’s safe, ensconced inside the vehicle.

The world is dying, Lucia thinks again, and it’s all our fault. What happened can’t be attributed to any one person—and it certainly can’t be attributed to Lucia. But it is the fault of generations of ignorant and oblivious humans, blind to the truth in front of them. It is the fault of those few in power who became wholly and absolutely corrupted, those who, in their lust for power, destroyed humanity once and for all.

While the world hadn’t collapsed all at once, it had been rotting for years now—long enough that Oliver, her son, had known little else. He turns to her, tears in his eyes. She hates to see him like this. She had spent years trying to shield him—kept him indoors, changed the stories on the news (back when there even was such a thing), told him stories of a cleaner world, one with blue skies and bluer oceans, green grass and a yellow sun.

Yet now he was seeing the lie behind them.

“Why are they so mad?” he sobs. To be honest, Lucia doesn’t know herself. But humans, she has learned, have a little bit of evil in them, and when things start to go wrong, that little bit can turn into a big, gnawing desire to hate. But Lucia can’t tell that to Oliver. At twelve, he’s already too smart for his own good and understands things that even she doesn’t. So she strokes his hair and murmurs into his ear that “It’s going to be alright,” even if she doesn’t believe it herself. She turns to the driver, a stern-faced man in his fifties. Looks like a man who's seen his fair share of battles—and to be fair, who hasn’t, nowadays?

“Excuse me, but where are you taking us?” Manners, she realizes. Somehow, even now, civility still exists. But she doesn’t get more than a grunt in response. The man had showed up at her house (more of a hovel, really) and flashed a government issued badge, then towed them into the car. What could the visit possibly be for? It had only been a couple months since that day, and the funeral—she shoves the thought out of her mind. No use dwelling on it. But, what if—no! No, no, no. She cuts the thought off sharply. They can’t take Oliver from her. They have replacements. It will be fine, she tells herself, forcing the thought into place. So she settles back, holding her son close and wishing for better days. But those days only exist in memory, now.
