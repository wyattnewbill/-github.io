This work aims to explore the human condition. I don’t know what it will inevitably morph into, but right now, as I put mental pen to paper and physical finger to key, the goal is simple: to interact with the discourse on what it means to be human; the boundary between the physical and metaphysical; what is, ultimately, the point of this futile existence—fully aware that there may be no answers or explanations, and believing that the act of grappling with the question is meaningful in and of itself.

If I’m being honest, this is a sort of ‘word vomit’—a way to give shape to the thoughts that creep in while lying awake at night, staring at the ceiling, wondering things that feel too big and too abstract to say out loud. Thoughts that plague me—plague everyone at some point in life. Questions that all humans—young or old, spry or frail, important or entirely insignificant—can’t help but ask of themselves and of the universe. For what are humans, after all, if not creatures of curiosity?

So let’s start with the most unavoidable question of all: why are we here? The question has been asked so often—by philosophers, theologians, scientists, insomniacs—that it risks sounding worn smooth. But sit with it for more than a moment and it refuses to behave. It splinters. It pulls apart into contradictions, assumptions, and half-answers, until it collapses into a far more unsettling question: how? How did a cloud of stardust turn into something that can think, love, grieve, and sit at a desk interrogating the universe as if it owes us an explanation?

Strangely enough, reducing why to how makes the problem both more complicated and far more interesting. Because to even begin answering it, we have to zoom out—way out. Past human history, past civilization, past Earth itself. We have to trace the story backward through time and space, beyond intuition and beyond understanding.

And that, inconveniently, is where things get weird.

What came before—and what comes after?

What came before—the chicken or the egg? It’s a question most disregard, toss aside as being no more than a joke, an irrelevant, pedantic curiosity. And yet, apply that same logic to the universe, and you have an entirely different beast altogether. What came before everything? When did the universe begin—and perhaps more unsettlingly, what will come after?

About 13.8 billion years ago, the universe popped into being with a spark, a flash, and a bang—the Big Bang, to be exact. This is of course, the most well-established and commonly held theory as to what actually happened (because, as no humans were alive to witness the moment the universe came to be, we can’t say for certain what events really transpired). The entire mass of the universe was combined into a mass smaller than, as Neil DeGrasse Tyson so marvelously put it, a trillionth the size of the period at the end of this sentence. As a matter of fact, it was so small as to be nearly impossible—it was a singularity, that itty-bitty destroyer of worlds, suns, star systems, and physics itself. Yet somehow, something lit the fuse, and in the space of one second, the universe grew to a few trillion miles wide, the fundamental particles that make up our reality coalesced into atoms, and the inevitable wheel of time was set in motion. Something I find particularly astonishing is that all matter owes its existence to a billion-to-one asymmetry in this early universe, where a tiny excess of hadrons escaped annihilation with their antimatter counterparts. Every galaxy you can see and every galaxy you cannot, every star, planet, and atom, owes its existence to that infinitesimal surplus. The vast cosmic structures that stretch across billions of light-years today are the remnants of a billionth of the universe’s original matter—which formed in a timespan shorter than a heartbeat.

Feeling overwhelmed yet? Because the Big Bang, for all its explosive spectacle, doesn’t actually answer the question—it’s just the bookend to it. Saying the universe began with the Big Bang is like beginning a story on chapter two. It tells us how the universe evolved from an early state, not why that state existed in the first place. The real question remains stubbornly untouched: what set the initial conditions? What, if anything, existed before time and space itself began?

Here’s where intuition fails us. “Before” is a word that assumes time, yet time itself emerged with the universe. Asking what came before the Big Bang is like asking what lies north of the North Pole—a question grammatically valid but physically meaningless. Our language, any human language, simply cannot express or even grasp the inexistence of time. And yet, the human mind refuses to let it go. If time began, it must have begun somehow. If the universe exists, it must have a reason…

Unless existence itself requires none.

You see, while we don’t know for certain what actually came before the Big Bang—largely because we lack a unified Theory of Everything, a quantum gravity that reconciles general relativity with quantum mechanics—scientists have a couple theories. These scientists call themselves ‘quantum cosmologists,’ and they are part of an exclusive club populated by some of the brightest minds in physics: Stephen Hawking, Andrei Linde, and Alexander Vilenkin have all wrestled with what happened at the very moment of the Big Bang, what—if anything—preceded it, and what the universe’s ultimate fate might be.

Vilenkin in particular has explored a frighteningly fascinating hypothesis: that of quantum fluctuations and their role in the birth of the universe. This theory posits that the universe quite literally materialized out of nothing at all—before the Big Bang, nothing existed; not time, not space, and certainly not matter. Even that word, ‘materialized,’ is unable to describe these quantum fluctuations properly. As Hawking described it, the universe “would be neither created nor destroyed. It would just BE.”

Such notions are simply incomprehensible to our limited human consciousness. Our language doesn’t even have the tools to describe such a thing. Thus, there really is no such thing as ‘before’ the universe existed—time didn’t exist until the universe did. (And yet, I just concluded that sentence with the past tense of ‘do…’ do you see the conundrum here?)

When we talk about quantum fluctuations, what we’re really talking about is quantum tunneling, where objects can instantly appear anywhere without actually traveling there in space. Quantum mechanics is a science based on probabilities, and it follows that subatomic particles behave as though they are in many places at once—it’s simply when we observe them that they settle into a definite position; think Schrodinger’s Cat. However, while quantum tunneling has been observed in the laboratory and is very possible on the micro scale, it is nigh impossible on the macro level, which we inhabit—that’s why the notion that the universe simply appeared seems ludicrous. But you must remember that at the moment of the Big Bang, the universe was microscopic, and smaller than any particles we know of. Thus, it is perfectly alright (to quantum physicists, at least) to posit that the universe just… appeared, without warning, finding its path out of that quantum murk that we call physics.

Such a theory has vast scientific, but more importantly, theological, philosophical, and social implications. Today, many believe that God is, as William Paley put it, a ‘divine watchmaker’—He created the universe, poised to start ticking away just as a watch might, wound it up, and let it go. Yet according to Hawking and Vilenkin, there is no reason for such a ‘watchmaker,’ when the universe could have easily wound itself up. The concrete rules of quantum mechanics—though it is in itself an impermanent and imperfect science—leave no room for a God. “What place, then, for a creator?” asks Hawking.

No place.

No place indeed.

Because if Hawking, Vilenkin, and others are right, then the most unsettling answer to the question of what came before the universe is this: nothing did. Not ‘nothing’ in the poetic sense—no dark void waiting patiently for creation—but nothing in the most literal, unforgiving way possible. It hurts to imagine, because it breaks the way in which we think about the way our universe works, the way our world works. There was no time in which something could happen. No space in which it could occur. No cause waiting to trigger an effect. Just a quantum haze of probabilities and possibilities.

In quantum physics, events do not need reasons in the way we expect them to. Electrons shift orbits without cause. Particles appear and vanish without explanation. Physicists feel no discontent in simply saying, ‘That’s the way it is’. Cause and effect—so fundamental to how we experience the world—begins to dissolve at the quantum level. According to Don Page, a leading cosmologist at the University of Alberta, causality itself may not be a law of the universe at all, but an approximation: a mental shortcut our brains evolved to make sense of a reality that is, at its core, far stranger than intuition allows. If that is true, then asking what caused the universe is the wrong question entirely. The universe may not have been caused. It may just be.

That possibility is deeply uncomfortable. Science, philosophy, religion, nearly every system of thought—as a matter of fact, our entire lives—are built on the assumption that things happen because of other things. I went to bed because I was tired. I danced because I was happy.

But pull that thread loose, and everything begins to unravel. If causality is not fundamental, then neither is inevitability. Responsibility, meaning, even morality begin to feel less solid, more phantom. The marble foundation cracks, and suddenly we’re not quite sure where to step, lest we fall into the depths of uncertainty. Isaac Newton—the most influential man of the millennium—famously said, “I do not know what I may appear to the world, but to myself I seem to have been only like a boy playing on the sea-shore, and diverting myself in now and then finding a smoother pebble or a prettier shell than ordinary, whilst the great ocean of truth lay all undiscovered before me.”

We often stand on the bedrock assumption that we are the end-all, be-all—that we are the pinnacle of all that is great in the universe. But what do we know, really? What is the yardstick which we measure ourselves by? Our self-assured view of ourselves as the center of the universe begins to falter when we consider the foundations on which it rests. What if the rules we assume—cause and effect, intention and consequence—are not as absolute as we believe?

Our understanding of the self begins to blur at the edges. We base who we are on the assumption that actions flow from causes: intentions, choices, character. But if the universe itself is not causally grounded, then neither are we, at least not in the way we imagine. Quantum mechanics says that everything is random—that all we are is probabilities, fluctuations in an otherwise silent world. If that’s true, then are we authors of our actions, or simply local patterns in a much larger, indifferent system? Is choice real, or is it just the sensation of choosing experienced by a brain obeying laws it never agreed to?

The unsettling answer may be: both. Or neither. Or something so far removed from our intuition that the question itself collapses.

And yet, despite how rarely we confront these questions, they hover constantly at the edges of our lives. Most of the time, we keep ourselves busy inside the small, manageable spheres of our existence. We eat. We sleep. We work. We love. We build things. We make art. But every so often—if we’re unlucky or curious enough—we look up. We wonder about the universe. Whether reality has an edge. Whether anything had to exist at all.

We can trace our own origins backward with remarkable precision: parents, grandparents, tens of thousands of ancestors stretching tens of thousands of years into the past; early humans; primates; single-celled organisms drifting in ancient seas; the formation of Earth; the slow collapse of gas into stars. It all happened, whether we think about it or not. And yet, no matter how far back we follow the chain, we eventually reach a point where the questions stop behaving. Where ‘before’ loses its meaning. Where explanation gives way to… nothing.

Perhaps that is where the question of origins must end—not with an answer, but with an admission of limitation. We are creatures suspended midway between atoms and galaxies, experiencing only a thin slice of reality, armed with language that breaks under the weight of the universe’s beginning. The fact that we can even glimpse these questions at all is, in its own way, astonishing.
