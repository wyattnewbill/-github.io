A HOME BEYOND

Through darkness we rode
Held our breath against the stars
Each carried such burdens!

Light as dawn bloomed—hope trembled
In shadow, still we built.
Greeted by night, we answered with flame
Hearth by hearth, a future formed

How peace is such a thin kindling!
Our brightest light cast shadows
Until darkness grew between us
Still and cold, that unending night

BEYOND, A HOME!

I thinkwrite these words in the dark of my cell on the Pharos. The great ship circles the planet on which the future of humanity rests. Or is that future already dead? I know these words offer little, but they are the only comfort I have—to know that somewhere, someone is reading these words. That someone may understand our failure so that they can continue. Whether another species or another group of humans, I do not know. Maybe there is another Lighthouse in that eternal black. But I do not concern myself with such philosophical and intangible thoughts. After all, I will die soon.

To understand my sentence, you must understand my goal. I was a proud member of the Lighthouse, the last great creation of humanity. Sent into the stars with a dying planet’s last breath, we were the Beacons to light the way into the next chapter of humanity. Our mission was simple: to create a new people, in a new civilization, on a new planet. A fresh start, without the stigmas and susceptibilities of the people Before. A light in the dark.

The Lighthouse.

Comprised of the greatest minds of our time, the Lighthouse was supposed to be perfect. But as I have well learned in these past weeks, perfection is a mirage—almost attainable, always seemingly within reach, but never truly there. It’s fake, another illusion created by humanity to fool the masses. We are doomed to fail, to destroy everything we touch. Loss and strife is as much a part of humanity as the love and kindness we claim to follow. One need only look at the Lighthouse to know humanity—how love can turn to hatred and how hope can just as easily turn to shame and resignation.

This is the tale of that Lighthouse, of the end of humanity, of the end of the good man. It is an account of our failure and success. I believe I am the last true human; the last true vestige of a dead people. It does not scare me as it once does, when I was young and full of life. I feel instead a strange peace; to know that likely, it is another race that will read these words. If you are indeed of another race, know the failures of our species so that yours may live. Know our fall, not from heaven, but from a heaven that became hell, into a much more terrifying reality.

Know the Lighthouse.

My name was Oliver. My mother, Lucia, named me after my grandfather. I don’t remember much of her — the constant deepsleeps take their toll, after a while. The first day on the Pharos, after launch, I cried for hours. Many of us did. But eventually, after hundreds of years and several deepsleeps, the memories blurred, faded through the fog of centuries. Now it’s no more than a dull ache that only comes every now and then, when my thoughts turn to my old home and the people, long-dead, I loved.

Those thoughts I would rather forget: yellow-brown skies choked by smoke and ash, an orange sun, screaming, rioting. Death and destruction. Some of the other Lights remember true Earth, the one with blue skies and bluer oceans, green grass and a yellow sun shining.

I don’t.

The pod opens with a hiss, followed by a faint whirring that drills into my skull. I’m still immobilized—the injection of post-deepsleep fluids, nutrients, and paralytics has done its work. My body is still encased in the slick biogel, holding me in place like liquid glass. Soon, though, electrical signals will pulse through the polymer, molecules loosening, shifting, liquefying, and freeing me at last from my centuries-long imprisonment.

I feel myself growing heavier as I am slowly released from the hold of the biogel—the weight of centuries pressing down, settling into my limbs like sediment in water. Anticipation, fear, awe, and disorientation swirl together, a knot I cannot untie. My muscles tremble and tense, rusty from lack of use, eager and reluctant at once.

The liquid drains with a soft gurgle, and the padded back of the pod cradles me as I am lowered. Gas hisses and curls through the chamber, steam swirling like smoke from those distant fires of my childhood. My chest heaves, lungs straining with the first breaths in decades. I blink furiously as electrodes and access ports peel away, retracting with clinical precision.

The first thing I notice is the cold, sharp and biting. My teeth chatter as the sterile light above sears through my closed eyelids. I shiver violently, muscles trembling like fragile wire.

Then, a shadow falls over the glow. Peredor’s face appears, grinning. He seems too warm in the antiseptic chillness of the chamber.

“Help me wake the others,” he whispers, voice low.

I nod, moving from pod to pod, hands careful, deliberate. I shake a few lightly awake, the soft groans and whispered curses of those first moments of consciousness echoing against the steel walls. Air ripples in response, a promise of life reborn.

Ithas is the last. He grins, wrapping me in a tight, awkward embrace, eyes glinting with wonder and disbelief. “Is it the day?” he asks, voice cracking after centuries of silence.

“I do not know,” I say, “but I expect that we will soon find out.”

From across the room, a familiar voice calls out: “Finally. I was beginning to think you’d all sleep forever.” Valen leans casually against his open pod, rubbing his eyes, hair still damp with the residue of the biogel. He smiles easily, the kind of smile that feels like sunlight after a long, dark night.

“Don’t be smug,” Ithas teases, nudging him.

“I’m not smug,” Valen responds, “just efficient.”

Peredor’s deep baritone echoes throughout the chamber. “Come, fellow Lights. A new candidate has been found.”

I look out the portside window, expecting the inky black of space. Instead, I stop short.

Hanging there beyond the glass is a world.

Clouds swirl slowly over its surface, pale and deliberate. Light spills across curved horizons. There is color—soft blues, muted greens, pastel purples, the faint suggestion of something alive. “Earth,” I whisper, my voice awed.

“No, but similar,” said Peredor, coming to stand by me. “It was called TRAPPIST-1e in the old days, but now it is nameless.”

Nameless, because names imply ownership—and we swore never to repeat that sin

Valen lets out a laugh—shocked and awed. “It actually worked,” he says, half to himself. “After all that… it worked.”

No one answers him. We are all afraid that if we speak, the image will vanish, that the universe will remember itself and correct the error.

Peredor breaks the silence. “Orbital insertion in twelve hours,” he says. “Surface surveys are already underway. There are regions suitable for landing. Regions suitable for permanence.”

Permanence. The word strikes me harder than any alarm ever could. I have lived my entire life in motion—between sleeps, between stars, between versions of humanity that no longer exist. Permanence is an idea from stories. From Earth.

From before.

The days that followed blurred together in a rush of purpose. That was the thing about the Lighthouse at the beginning—we were very good at purpose. We moved like a single organism, each mind a nerve ending, each decision reflexive and clean. Cities unfolded in holograms. Agricultural lattices grew across virtual plains. Governance models were argued, discarded, resurrected, refined. We spoke of balance, of restraint, of learning from the corpses of our old mistakes.

We spoke beautifully.

When the first lander descended, I watched from the observation deck with my hands pressed flat against the glass. The atmosphere flared white around the hull, then orange, then a deep, trembling red. For a moment—just a moment—it looked like the ship was burning, like Earth all over again. I tasted bile and smelled ash that wasn’t there.

But the flames thinned. The signal stabilized.

They made planetfall at 03:17 ship time.

Cheers erupted. Some of us cried openly. Ithas sank to his knees and whispered something I couldn’t hear. Peredor closed his eyes, head bowed, as if in prayer. Valen hugged the nearest person—me—and laughed again. Reality had sunk in.

We named the first settlement Dawn.

That should have been the end of the story. Or at least the beginning of a gentler one.

But perfection, as I said, is a mirage. And like fools, we mistook its glow for light.
