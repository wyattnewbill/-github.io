Words are what drive humanity. They are what lift us from the dirt and raise our eyes to the skies. Without them, we are animals, relegated to the dust.

Words are what make us human. And to be human is to dream—to have joy, love, the emotions that make us apes undeniably different from any other animal on the planet. And what are words on a page but dreams come to life? Writing is simply dreaming in another state, another form. To write is to dream.

The first dream I remember wasn’t something I saw—rather, something I spoke. I was three years old, sitting cross-legged on the plush rug that covered the living room with a book far too big for my hands. The letters were small, strange shapes—mysterious symbols adults wielded with casual mastery while I stared, envious, at those locked doors. But that day, one of the doors cracked open. I sounded out the first word: “M-O-N-I-C-A.”

My mother gasped. I didn’t. Yet something in me sensed that a world had shifted, that something had been set into motion, as irreversible and unmovable as those laws that govern our existence. I had discovered the first key to the universe.

Reading blossomed after that, and as the number of books I read increased, so did my love of reading. I read anything and everything I could unlock with my little key. Words were everywhere, and they filled my life with texture, color, depth. They offered me a thousand lives beyond my own. Whether I wanted to be a knight in shining armor or a superhero flying through the sky, a story was always waiting to give me that life, even if only for a short while.

But the moment words truly changed me happened in the library—that world of worlds, refuge of dreams and ideas. I pulled a battered copy of Percy Jackson from a low shelf, opened it, and suddenly I wasn’t in the library anymore. I was at Camp Half-Blood, sword in hand, terrified and brave all at once.

When I closed the book, the air felt wrong, like I’d been dropped back into reality too abruptly. I had just been fighting monsters and myths—how could everybody be so calm and everything so normal? I realized then that stories weren’t only an escape. They were more—a life, a portal to another world, a form of magic adults passed off, as they did so many other things, as simply that. They were a dream.

And with a love of that dream came a love of the story: a love of the art of putting pen to paper, creating something from nothing, turning ideas from abstract thoughts into tangible, physical representations of the dream. The story was an escape from the trials and tribulations of daily life. It offered anything and everything.
