The man was rich. Filthy rich, stupid rich, crazy rich. Rich enough that the word lost all meaning. He inherited a portion from his long-dead parents, who showered him with lots of gifts and little time; earned a good portion through marriage—though it wasn’t happy nor fulfilling, given that he had married for her beauty and wealth, not love; made some through honest dealings using his company; and stole a majority with dishonest dealings. In short, the man was unscrupulous, and quite frankly, rotten to the core.

The woman was poor—at least compared to the man, and most anybody would be poor compared to the man. She worked as a receptionist at his company, greeting people with a smile that always came of her own volition. She led a simple life, did not jet around the world nor own six homes on four continents. And while she did not have what the man had, she was content in her own way—with a loving husband, three loving children and in-laws, a modest house, and a well of kindness that never seemed to run out. It can be inferred that the woman was kind, and quite frankly, enchanting both inside and out.

But no matter what happened over the course of their very long lives, the woman and the man were both taken by death—the great equalizer—eventually. In fact, they both died on the same day, at the same time—to the minute. The man was attended by his doctors, who marked the time of his passing and the way in which he passed—the man had no children, and his wife was long-dead. The woman was attended by her husband, and her three children, as well as two of her grand-children and all three of her in-laws—and, of course, her doctors, who marked the time of her passing and the way in which she passed.

The man died assuming an all-encompassing nothing—the woman died assuming the same. Instead, they both arrived, alone, at the same place—a staircase—with the same entity—a kindly being with eyes that sparkled with amusement and sadness and who was dressed in all white—next to them.

But before they could voice their questions, or even form sentences, the being spoke, and this is what the being said, or at least the gist of it: “I am not a god, nor a devil. I am not an angel, nor a demon. I am not Death, nor Life. I simply am.”

As it spoke, the man and woman glanced at the staircase most familiar to them—to the man, the marbled steps of his childhood home, to the woman, the narrow wooden staircase of her home.

The being smiled faintly, as if it could see their thoughts—and perhaps it could. “The staircase is the measure of your life. Each step carries a voice—what was said of you and what you left behind. The cruel will come first, the kind later. You will see in all its glory and shame what you have wrought with the life you were given.

“You may stop, but never turn back. There is no escape from the staircase—no escape from death. What can be found at the bottom of the staircase is a secret only known to those who pass through those hallowed walls. This all things shall know, and thusly come to pass.”

And with those words, the being disappeared, faded from existence much as the man and woman had—with a sigh and a look of, if not peace, then a gentle resignation.

The man hesitated in the silence. The woman took a breath. And they began.
