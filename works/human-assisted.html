The first time I used ChatGPT, I couldn’t find a quote from Harry Potter. Fifteen minutes later, I had 5 different quotes and an outline on how to refine my writing. AI didn’t replace my thinking—it helped enhance it.

That’s what people misunderstand about AI. It’s not a replacement for creativity—it’s a reflection of it. Sure, sometimes I’ll use AI to shorten an interminable task, but its real use is to build on what you already have—to brainstorm, learn, and clarify. AI doesn’t think for me; it helps me think better.

That’s because, at the heart of the controversy surrounding AI, there lies a truth that many people miss about it: AI is, at its most fundamental form, a tool, not a replacement. Like a hammer or even the device you’re reading this on, it exists to solve problems and improve our lives. Yes, it’s a more complex and controversial tool, but the premise is the same. Just as you can use a hammer to build or destroy, you can use AI to cheat or learn, to outsource your thinking or expand it. The way we use our tools matters more than the tools themselves.

My generation is the first to grow up with AI as a presence, not a concept—to learn, change, and become human in the age of the machine. I’ve used it to study for the SAT, clarify confusing math problems, and turn messy ideas into clear plans. Long nights spent poring over a textbook are now turned into conversations that help me understand and retain the material better. Teachers at my school use it to save time grading, which means more energy spent on teaching. That’s what this technology does best—it clears space and gives us more time to think, learn, and create.

In the real world, it’s helping people achieve the impossible faster: students coding websites and apps overnight, scientists finding medical breakthroughs, writers outlining and editing and writing ten times faster. The barriers between idea and action are thinner than ever.

Of course, there’s fear. Many lambast the scary aspects of AI, and rightly so; it’s changing the way we learn, think, and act—sometimes for the worse. But every major leap in human history—fire, printing presses, the internet, even—brought a fear of the unknown.

What AI threatens is not creativity itself but our definition of it. We often imagine creativity as something solitary and pure, the lone genius with a blank page. But that has never been true. Creativity has always been collaborative—between mentors and students, artists and audiences, humans and their tools. Paintbrushes expanded what hands could express. Cameras expanded what eyes could capture. Computers expanded what minds could calculate. AI expands what we can imagine.

Emerson said ‘to be great is to be misunderstood,’ and the same applies to the machine. AI isn’t the harbinger of the end of human creativity—it’s an opportunity to redefine it.

This isn’t the end of human creativity. It’s the next chapter of it.

A chapter we get to write—human; assisted.
