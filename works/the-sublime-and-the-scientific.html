Abstract

Astronomical phenomena have long inspired experiences of awe, wonder, and terror—the sublime—yet science fiction and science represent these phenomena in fundamentally different ways. This paper asks how science fiction transforms the astronomical sublime and how its representations differ from those found in traditional science. Drawing on the philosophies of the sublime developed by Burke, Kant, and contemporary researchers, this paper will conduct a comparative analysis of scientific and fictional representations of astronomical phenomena. Through close reading of Galileo’s Sidereus Nuncius, Neil deGrasse Tyson’s Astrophysics for People in a Hurry, NASA’s Hubble Imagery, Olaf Stapledon’s Star Maker, Arthur C. Clarke’s 2001: A Space Odyssey, and Christopher and Jonathan Nolan’s Interstellar, this paper argues that science fiction does not merely reproduce the astronomical sublime but transforms it by introducing human narratives and existential uncertainty that science, in its pursuit of explanation, minimizes. Thus, while scientific representations cultivate awe through knowledge and visual vastness, science-fiction preserves the terror and emotional vulnerability of space to embed cosmic phenomena within human experiences.

Introduction

In April of 2005, NASA released two images to mark the fifteenth anniversary of the Hubble Space Telescope. The first was the Eagle Nebula—towering columns of dust and gas suffused with orange and teal light, rising towards the heavens like geological formations carved by forces of the divine. The second was the Whirlpool Galaxy, a luminous spiral drawing the eye inwards along pathways of blue and red towards its brilliant golden core. Those two images would appear on calendars and coffee mugs, museum walls and magazine covers around the world. People who had never studied astronomy and could not have named a single extrasolar system nonetheless recognized in these images something that exceeded their capacity of articulation. Elizabeth Kessler, who has written the definitive study of Hubble imagery, notes that viewers consistently described images as “awe-inspiring,” “stunning,” and even “sublime” (Kessler 63). The images seemed to exceed the viewer's capacity to metabolize them—to place the human observer before something so vast, ancient, and indifferent to human existence that every category of description failed.

That response has both a name and a long philosophical history. The experience of encountering something that overwhelms ordinary comprehension—that produces a strange, breathtaking pleasure of terror and wonder—is what aesthetic philosophers have termed the sublime. Edmund Burke (1757) identified astonishment—the suspension of reason before an overwhelming experience—as the sublime’s defining feature. Immanuel Kant (1790) reframed the encounter into a conflict between reason and imagination: while the imagination fails, reason ultimately asserts its superiority and converts overwhelm into understanding. It is not incidental, then, that both accounts were absorbed by Romanticism, the defining literary and philosophical movement of the 18th and 19th centuries (1780-1830) that made the individual’s encounter with the vast scope of nature—Wordsworth’s Alps, Coleridge’s icy seas, Shelley’s towering mountains—its central subject. The tension between these two—Burke and Kant, terror and triumph—has divided the conversation on the sublime ever since, and it will underlie this paper’s argument as well.

A keener version of the sublime—the astronomical sublime—is that sublime which, in the Burkean tradition, is vast, indifferent, and beyond the mind’s capacity to understand. What makes this philosophical tradition urgent now is that human beings encounter the astronomical sublime through two very different modes. Scientific representation—telescopic imagery, astrophysics, scientific communication—gives us the cosmos through the vernacular of data, explanation, a position of rational mastery that converts astonishment into understanding. Science fiction gives us something different. In science fiction, the encounter with the cosmos is not resolved into understanding, but remains a crisis—existential, overwhelming, and unresolved. As science fiction increasingly incorporates rigorous scientific content—Christopher Nolan's consultation with physicist Kip Thorne in the production of Interstellar being an obvious case—the question of what each mode can and cannot do with the experience of cosmic vastness becomes practically significant as well as theoretically interesting.

This brings the paper to its central research question: how does science fiction capture or transform the sublime awe associated with astronomical phenomena, and how does it differ from representations of the sublime in astronomical science? The hypothesis is as such: while astronomy constructs the sublime through empirical representations of the cosmos, science fiction extends the astronomical sublime by placing human experience within that vastness. Through narrative techniques and character-driven story, science fiction transforms scientific awe into existential confrontation, preserving the terror, wonder, and mystery of the unknown even as science seeks to explain it.

The significance of this argument extends in several directions. It offers science fiction studies a way of understanding the genre's philosophical ambition that goes beyond its relationship to scientific prediction or social commentary. It identifies a structural feature of scientific representation—its drive toward resolving the sublime—that has implications for how scientists narrate the cosmos to public audiences. And it tests the major theories of the sublime against two representational modes that, taken together, span the full range from Kantian resolution to Burkean irresolution. The paper proceeds by establishing, in Section I, the theoretical framework; examining, in Sections II and III, scientific and SF representations through close readings of Galileo’s Sidereus Nuncius, Neil deGrasse Tyson’s Astrophysics for People in a Hurry, NASA’s Hubble and JWST Imagery, Olaf Stapledon’s Star Maker, Arthur C. Clarke’s 2001: A Space Odyssey, and Christopher and Jonathan Nolan’s Interstellar; and drawing, in the conclusion, the corpus into a single argument about what these two representations reveal about the sublime.
