There’s a theory in physics called String Theory: it postulates that the universe is made up of tiny, endlessly vibrating strings. And if there are strings that have tied my universe together?

It’s words.

I don’t remember deciding I wanted to be a writer. I remember realizing, much later, that I’d been becoming one for years.

My first book was at 3, on the Pororo mat of my childhood home, as my dad sounded out the words with me. My first library visit was at 4, picking the cool-looking space books off the shelves. My first movie was at 4, nestled on the couch between my parents. I didn’t know it then, but I had discovered the first string of the universe.

I remember watching Monty Python and the Holy Grail with my father on a weekend night, on the couch. We laughed for two hours straight until we were holding our bellies, sore but happy. We still quote that one to each other, even years later.

I remember watching The Martian with my mother and father a couple months after it came out on DVD. I went to my room when Mark Watney pried the communications antenna out of his own abdomen in a hasty surgery, but came back when he started to—in his own words—science the \*\*\*\* out of Mars. I wanted to be like him.

For a while, I wanted to be a lot of things—astronaut, archaeologist, astrophysicist, engineer. I used to watch the YouTube channel The Hacksmith not because I wanted to learn the intricacies of physical mechanics but because I just thought it was so cool that using nothing but their brains, these people could bring things from movies into the real world. But those weren’t me: I didn’t want to spend my life in G-force machines, or in libraries memorizing ancient battles, and math? Don’t even think about it. Writing survived as an ambition out of everything else because it is at the core of who I am and the way I see the world. Creativity and the idea of building something from purely my mind came naturally to me—it flowed through me.

Now? I wonder at how they wrote the intricate science into the screenplay of The Martian, and every time I watch the surgery scene, I wonder how the special effects and makeup teams might have made it look so real on the big screen.

I wonder at what the writers room looked like for Monty Python.

I wonder at how Andy Weir made the science behind The Martian integrate so seamlessly into the text.

I wonder at how Pierce Brown crafted such effortless prose for Red Rising, how he crafted such intricate worlds and such real people.

I wonder… I wonder.

When something appears in my mind—an image, a line of dialogue, even a feeling—I feel compelled to write it down. I think the best analogy for it is that my mind is like a rushing river, flowing over a rocky riverbed. There are rocks, granite and diorite, in that riverbed—and there are gems too: iron veins, gold veins, even diamonds and rubies and emeralds in the rough. Those are my ideas. Most of the time, the water is high and fast-flowing, and the ideas are hidden in my subconscious, no matter how much they may glitter. But sometimes an idea comes popping up to the surface, and in that moment, I can never think straight. I need to take that rock out of the water, leave it to stand on its own. When an idea pops up to the surface, I must write it down. I must. And that is that.

Writing is my way to give back to the people that created me—both the authors and the characters—and it is my way to give forwards to the people that, with a little bit of luck, I can shape too. I once heard writing compared to pitching a tent on a campground. Every writer who has come before you has pitched their own tent, and you can’t avoid pitching your tent on top of another’s. Every time I pitch my tent, I’m having a conversation with those who, in the past, explored the same ideas—and I’m leaving my own tent behind for other writers to pitch their tents on top of in the future. It is my way to pour pieces of myself into vessels that will outlast me, to build stories that might persist, that might carry some fragment of who I am into the future.

It isn’t always easy. Writing is hard, and sometimes it feels like everything that comes out of me is worthless, and sometimes nothing will come out at all. But I think that in life, we get to choose our hard, and I choose writing as mine because it is the most rewarding to me. When I can lean back in my chair and smile at the work I’ve done, I feel fulfilled. When I can hear other people say that they were strung along by my words, that they wanted to see where the story went, I no longer care that it was hard writing the story. I wrote it, and it made someone feel something, and that’s what matters.

Writing lets us live a thousand lives in a single one—because to know these strings and their struggles and what makes them who they are, to know them, truly know them, to ‘walk a mile in their shoes’, as the saying goes, is the closest thing humanity has to magic. Without it, we are creatures of instinct and fear. With it, we are capable of compassion, understanding, and hope.

Words are strings: fragile, flickering things that make pieces of people come to life and teach us what it means to be human. They let us step into lives not our own and return changed. My universe began with a single word breathed into a quiet room, a tiny string that split open the world and let the light in. From that moment on, I’ve lived with one foot in reality and one foot in my universe—and I will never close that door.
